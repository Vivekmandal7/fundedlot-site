How to Pass a Prop Firm Evaluation: The Plan Most Traders Skip

Guides 21 October 2026· 8 min read· By the FundedLot team

Most traders approach a prop firm evaluation the way they approach a demo account: open the chart, look for something good, see what happens. The evaluation is a different kind of test. It does not ask whether you can find a good trade; it asks whether you can avoid three specific numbers for twenty or thirty trading days in a row. This is the plan that treats it that way — from the rulebook to the daily routine, and the moment when a second pair of eyes is worth paying for.

Step 1 — Reduce the rulebook to three numbers

Every two-step evaluation, whatever the firm, comes down to a profit target, a daily loss limit and a maximum drawdown. On a $100,000 account with the commonly used 8% / 5% / 10% set, that is: make $8,000; never be down more than $5,000 on a day; never let equity touch $90,000. Write the three numbers on a card and keep it next to the screen. If you cannot recite them, you are trading against rules you have not learned.

Then check the two details that change everything about how those numbers behave. First, is the daily limit measured on balance or on equity? Nearly every firm uses equity, which means an open loss counts the moment it exists, not when you close it. Second, is the drawdown static or trailing? A trailing floor follows your best equity upward and never comes back down, so a good week raises the line you must not cross. Drawdown types explained walks through both with numbers.

Step 2 — Size every trade from the daily limit, not from conviction

Here is the arithmetic that decides most evaluations before the first trade. Divide the daily limit by the risk you put on one trade and you get the number of losers you can take today. At $500 of risk against a $5,000 limit, ten straight losers end the day. At $2,000, the third one does. Pick that divisor on purpose, in advance, and write it on the same card.

A personal daily stop at about half the firm's limit is the single most effective habit we see in accounts that survive. Stopping yourself at −$2,500 leaves a full buffer for tomorrow; using the whole limit bets the attempt on your worst hour. Position sizing from the daily loss limit has a worked table for the common account sizes.

Step 3 — Treat Phase 1 and Phase 2 as different exams

Phase 1 is usually the larger target in a shorter window; Phase 2 is a smaller target with the same loss rules and, often, no deadline at all. Traders who pass Phase 1 on a hot streak frequently fail Phase 2 by trading the same size when the mathematics now rewards patience. The second step is covered in detail in the Phase 2 guide; the short version is that your edge per trade does not need to change, your trades per day usually should.

Step 4 — Build one session, one routine

A typical evaluation is twenty to forty trades. Spread across six markets and three sessions, no market gets enough repetitions to reveal whether you have an edge in it — or a leak. Pick one session you can actually attend (London or New York for most forex and index traders) and one or two instruments, and make the routine identical every day:

  1. Before the open: the three numbers, today's risk per trade, the maximum number of trades (three is enough for most intraday plans).
  2. During: the plan's setups only. A setup has an entry zone, an invalidation level and a target written down before the order exists. If you cannot name the invalidation, there is no trade.
  3. After: ten minutes in the journal. On a sample this small, one recurring mistake is the entire gap between passing and paying for another attempt.

Step 5 — Rehearse the exact rules before you pay for them

Commonly cited industry estimates put first-attempt failure rates near 90%, and most fees are not refundable until you pass. The cheapest way to learn what your habits do under a daily loss limit is to run the identical rule set on virtual funds until ten attempts are on record. One attempt is noise; ten is a pass rate. A rule-enforced simulator such as FundedLot exists for this, and practicing challenges free explains how to make the rehearsal predictive rather than comforting.

Step 6 — Know when a second pair of eyes pays for itself

Some failures are information problems: you did not know the drawdown was trailing, you did not size from the limit. The steps above fix those. Other failures are pattern problems: you know the rule, you can recite it, and you still break it on the fourth trade of a red day. Pattern problems rarely fix themselves, because the person who made the pattern is the person reviewing it.

This is where a mentor who has passed the same kind of evaluation earns the fee. A good one does three things a chart cannot: posts setups as zones with an invalidation and a target, so you practise deciding rather than following; reviews your own trades against your own plan every week; and tells you, from your journal, which single habit to fix next. It is also what FundedLot Mentor is built to do, with the app's own mistake record feeding the review. Whichever room you join, apply the test in do you need a mentor to pass?: zones not prices, every outcome posted including the losers, and any pass count shown next to the number of members it came from.

The checklist

  • Three numbers on a card: target, daily limit, drawdown floor — and whether the floor trails.
  • Risk per trade chosen from the daily limit; a personal stop at half of it.
  • One session, one or two instruments, a cap on trades per day.
  • Every trade has a written entry zone, invalidation and target before the order.
  • Same-day journal; one habit to fix at a time.
  • Ten rehearsed attempts before one paid attempt.
  • If the same mistake survives three journals, get a reviewer who is not you.

Passing is not a single good month. It is twenty to thirty days of not doing the thing you usually do on a bad afternoon. Plan for the afternoon, and the month takes care of itself.
